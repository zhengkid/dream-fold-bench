Dream-Fold-Bench

Can Coding Agents Fold Proteins on Their Own?

Given an amino acid sequence, a general-purpose coding agent has three hours, one CPU core and a terminal to produce an all-atom structure, using only general-purpose software and the code it writes during the session. It may neither call nor train a neural network. We evaluate 12 frontier coding agents from Anthropic, Google and OpenAI on 53 CASP16 targets, with sequence and structure databases (open-book) and without them (closed-book).

Watch a session

How an agent folds a protein

Every session keeps a trace: each command, each version of the model, and the reports of its tools. Below, two open-book sessions of Claude Fable 5.2 are replayed from their traces, one for a target without a template in the Protein Data Bank and one with a remote homologue.

The grey trace is the experimental structure of the assessed part of the chain. The ribbon is coloured by how far each residue is from its experimental position after a superposition of the kind GDT_TS uses. The passage from one version to the next is interpolated for display.

Without a template · T1274

The search returns nothing to copy, and the session builds its own model: it derives contacts from coevolution, folds a chain of Cα atoms by annealing, borrows the pairing of strands from a distant family, and fits a backbone to the result. The first model, after 45 minutes, scores 22. Two revisions of how the strands pair and of how the domain is fitted take it to 46; from then on the versions polish that fold and stay there. AlphaFold 3 scores 100 on this target.

With a remote homologue · T1278

The session searches the archive and the sequence collections first, settles which templates to trust in which region, and writes its first model after 45 minutes. That model scores 84. The session then tests another alignment, rebuilds a loop from fragments and relaxes the model with a force field, 7 versions in all; the delivered model scores 85. The later work is careful, and it moves the score by a point: the backbone is where the template put it.

The benchmark

    
One sequence, three hours, no neural network

    
Dream-Fold-Bench evaluates general-purpose coding agents on protein structure prediction. A shared evaluation framework
      enforces the resource limits, records the execution traces, and scores the predicted structures against experimentally determined

Task and targets

        
We identified 60 CASP16 (2024) targets that fit the task of predicting the tertiary structure of a single protein chain, and
          excluded seven after a bio-safety review. The remaining 53 targets cover 75 official CASP16 evaluation units. The agent
          must submit one chain with all heavy atoms for every residue of the sequence.

      
Tools and feedback

        
3D visual feedback: a renderer draws any candidate structure from several viewpoints as an image the agent can look at
          (below). Evaluator: stereochemical checks and an energy computed with OpenMM (Amber ff14SB, GBn2 implicit solvent);
          we also evaluate feedback from the Rosetta ref2015 energy function.

      
Post-session scoring

        
After the session, the final model.pdb is compared with the experimental structure. Scoring follows the
          official CASP16 evaluation units; separately assessed domains of one submission are scored independently. GDT_TS is the primary
          metric; we also report TM-score and lDDT.

      
Session isolation

        
Each session runs in its own sandbox. The agent can call the language model, but has no access to the internet or to
          provider-side tools. Reference structures, scoring code, scores and the files of other sessions stay outside the sandbox.

      
Agents

        
12 models in three agent harnesses: Claude Code (Claude Fable 5.2, Fable 5.1, Opus 5.5, Opus 5, Sonnet 5.5), Gemini CLI
          (Gemini 4 Neon, Argon, Carbon, Hydrogen; Gemini 3.8 Flash, 3.1 Pro) and Codex CLI (GPT-6.2 Sol).

      
Runs and reporting

        
A run is one session per target under one agent, database setting and evaluator: 53 sessions. The main results rest on
          71 runs (3,763 sessions). A run's score is the mean over the 75 units; we report the mean across runs.
          A session that ends because of a server or infrastructure error is rerun once.

    


    

      

        
3D visual feedback: the agent can look at its structure

        
A coding agent works in a terminal, but a protein structure is a three-dimensional object. Every session therefore has a
          renderer: view/view.py FILE.pdb draws a candidate structure from the front, the top and the side,
          together with its contact map, saves the picture as an image file the agent can open and look at, and prints a text summary.
          The agent decides when to look. 10 of the 12 agents call the renderer in at least 85% of
          their open-book sessions.

Results

    
What the experiments show

    
Beyond the leaderboard: the agents are template modellers, the score of a session rises early and then levels off, and
      the feedback available during a session says little about which model is closer to the truth.


    

      

        
1 · Accuracy follows template availability

        
We group the 53 targets by their closest sequence match among the PDB chains released before the target (MMseqs2,
          sensitivity 7.5, E-value ≤ 0.001; the best hit maximises identity × coverage). A target's score is the mean over its evaluation
          units, and a type's score the unweighted mean over its targets.

3 · The feedback cleans up a model; it does not tell which model is right

        
4 agents were also run with an evaluator based on the Rosetta energy function in place of geometry + physics. Switching
          changes the mean GDT_TS by at most 0.5 points closed-book and 1.5 points open-book, in both directions,
          with no consistent advantage for either evaluator. Removing the evaluator altogether changes as little: in a control of
          3 open-book runs each, Claude Fable 5.2 scores 56.6 ± 0.7 without an evaluator and 56.9 ± 0.2 with geometry + physics; Claude Sonnet 5.5 scores 53.4 ± 1.8 without an evaluator and 52.7 ± 1.7 with geometry + physics.

      

      

      
Mean GDT_TS ± standard deviation across runs; superscript: number of runs. Difference: against geometry + physics.

      

        
Why does the kind of feedback matter so little? For every signal an evaluator reports, we ask how often it prefers the better of
          two models. Between two attempts by the same agent in the same setting whose scores differ by at least five GDT_TS points, the 8 signals pick
          the model with the higher GDT_TS in 46% to 63% of pairs, where 50% is chance.

4 · What the sessions do

        
No method was prescribed. In the open-book setting every agent arrives at the same one, the template-based modelling that the
          field used before neural networks: search the archive and the sequence databases, align, build, relax, and check.

Paper

    
Dream-Fold-Bench: Can Coding Agents Fold Proteins on Their Own?

    
We introduce Dream-Fold-Bench, a benchmark that asks coding agents to predict all-atom structures
      under fixed time budgets without using auxiliary neural networks. We evaluate 12 frontier coding agents from Google, OpenAI
      and Anthropic on CASP16 targets, with access to sequence and structure databases in the open-book setting and without access to those
      databases in the closed-book setting. The best agent, Claude Fable 5.2, achieves a mean GDT_TS of 56.9 in the open-book
      setting and 24.1 in the closed-book setting. With database access, agents approach AlphaFold 3 on targets with close
      structural templates, while their mean GDT_TS falls below half of AlphaFold 3's on targets for which template search finds no match.
      The feedback from Rosetta and from a traditional force field barely outperforms random guessing when selecting the better of two
      attempts by the same agent. Together, these results show that current coding agents perform best when close structural templates are
      available, and that their feedback signals offer limited guidance for selecting among candidate structures.
